From Carnival to the End of the World — managed as a product.

The implementation story behind a five-week, four-country journey: how one childhood ambition shaped prioritization, route architecture, trade-offs and a Scrum-inspired delivery model.

01 / Executive summary

The product was the experience, not the itinerary.

Experiencing Brazilian Carnival was a childhood dream. I wanted to make it happen without reducing South America to a single city break. Over approximately five weeks, the journey would combine Carnival in São Paulo and Rio, Uruguay's coast, Buenos Aires, Patagonia, Iguazú Falls and a day in Paraguay. The challenge was to protect one fixed-date experience while balancing all the others against travel time, connection dependencies and the freedom to explore.

This case study presents that real trip through a Product Owner lens. The itinerary and photographs are genuine portfolio evidence; the backlog, sprint sequence and management artifacts are a retrospective, Scrum-inspired reconstruction—not a claim that I operated a formal Scrum team during my holiday.

02 / Product vision

One fixed-date goal, competing opportunities.

The product vision was a South American journey anchored around Carnival, combining festival energy with cities, coastlines, mountains and waterfalls. The first prioritization decision was simple: Carnival was essential. Other experiences could be ordered around it, adapted or, if necessary, removed.

How could I maximize the experiences that mattered, while protecting the primary reason for travelling?

Berlin → São Paulo → Rio de Janeiro → Montevideo → Punta del Este → Buenos Aires → Ushuaia → Buenos Aires → Iguazú Falls → Ciudad del Este → São Paulo → Berlin

Two decisions made the route more practical: Punta del Este and Ciudad del Este were day trips rather than new overnight bases, and Buenos Aires was deliberately visited twice around the Ushuaia detour.

03 / Discovery and prioritization

From a wish list to a product backlog.

I can reconstruct the planning logic by distinguishing non-negotiable outcomes from valuable features and optional discoveries. The most important dependencies were the Carnival window, an executable cross-country route and the final international departure. Rio sightseeing, regional contrasts and Patagonia provided high experience value; individual dining discoveries remained flexible.

04 / Implementation

Seven proposed increments, one continuous journey.

Sprint 0

Discover the product goal

I began with Carnival as the non-negotiable reason for travel, then defined the desirable contrasts—coast, cities, southern wilderness and waterfalls—within a roughly five-week window. Increment: product vision and initial priorities.

Sprint 1

Design the route architecture

I sequenced the four countries and chose where to stay versus where to make day trips. Montevideo became the base for Punta del Este; Buenos Aires was split around the southern detour. Increment: a coherent route with fewer unnecessary hotel changes.

Sprint 2

Prepare critical dependencies

A realistic planning baseline prioritizes Carnival timing, major transport legs, accommodation, travel documents and a protected homeward connection. The portfolio does not preserve historical booking dates, prices or confirmations, so this increment documents the planning requirements rather than inventing them. Increment: actionable travel readiness.

Sprint 3

Deliver the Carnival anchor

The journey opened with three nights in São Paulo, followed by twelve days in Rio. A longer Rio stay made room for the celebrations, Christ the Redeemer, Ipanema's coastline and sunset, and an unexpected buffet discovery. Increment: the main reason for the journey, plus valuable experiences beyond it.

Sprint 4

Move from Carnival to city-and-coast contrast

Three days in Uruguay brought a slower rhythm. A bus day trip reached Punta del Este without relocating. I then continued via Colonia del Sacramento by bus and ferry, arriving to a clear view of Buenos Aires' skyline. I spent three days in the city before the journey south. Increment: Uruguay and the first Buenos Aires chapter.

Sprint 5

Deliver Patagonia, Iguazú and the return

In Ushuaia, three days included its waterfront shipwreck, the hike to Laguna Esmeralda and a Beagle Channel journey with a lighthouse and penguin colonies. After a further three days in Buenos Aires, the route continued to Iguazú Falls and a day trip to Ciudad del Este. Three final nights in São Paulo completed the route before returning to Berlin. Increment: the southern and northern adventure stages, followed by a practical end to the journey.

Sprint 6

Inspect results and publish the case

The photographs and original route create an evidence base for reviewing what was actually experienced. I have translated the journey into a public backlog, proposed sprint plan, risk table and implementation narrative. Increment: an inspectable independent Product Owner case study.

05 / Product decisions

The trade-offs behind the route.

Protect the primary value

Carnival came first. The other destinations had to fit around a fixed event, rather than compromise it.

Reduce unnecessary complexity

Punta del Este and Ciudad del Este worked as day trips; I gained two experiences without two additional overnight relocations.

Use hubs deliberately

Two Buenos Aires stays made the Ushuaia detour a practical part of a larger northbound route.

Reserve room for discovery

Twelve days in Rio allowed planned sights and unscheduled value, including repeated visits to Ipanema.

08 / Retrospective

What I learned about product ownership.

The biggest lesson is that good planning starts with the outcome, not the number of places on a map. Carnival provided the product goal. The itinerary then became a set of choices that supported it: which connections were necessary, which destinations deserved longer stays, which activities could fit into a day trip and where flexibility mattered more than rigid scheduling.

Keep: anchor ambitious journeys around one clear goal; prioritize fixed dependencies; use efficient bases and leave room for memorable discoveries. Improve: record actual cost baselines, booking decisions, buffers and changes in a lightweight decision log. Experiment next time: use a rolling 48-hour plan and a short review at each major destination, applying inspection and adaptation without unnecessary bureaucracy.

Portfolio takeaway. This case demonstrates product vision, outcome-based prioritization, dependency thinking, scope trade-offs and clear stakeholder communication. It complements my public-sector PMO case study, which focuses on portfolio governance and executive decision support.
