# From Carnival to the End of the World
## An independent Product Owner case study: implementing a five-week South American journey

**Product Owner:** Sumit Milan  
**Scope:** Brazil, Uruguay, Argentina and Paraguay  
**Duration:** Five weeks  
**Product goal:** Fulfil a childhood ambition to experience Brazilian Carnival while delivering a feasible, varied journey across four countries.  
**Approach:** A retrospective, Scrum-inspired analysis of a real trip—not a claim that a staffed Scrum team or formal Scrum events operated during the journey.

### 1. The problem: one dream, many competing options

Carnival was the reason I wanted to go to South America. But simply arriving in Rio for a few nights would have left a larger opportunity unexplored. I wanted time in Brazil, the slower pace of Uruguay, the urban character of Buenos Aires, the extraordinary southern landscapes around Ushuaia and the power of Iguazú Falls. With around five weeks available, these ambitions were competing for time, transport capacity, attention and money.

The fundamental product decision was therefore not *how many cities can I visit?* It was *which experiences justify the limited time, and in what order can I realistically deliver them?* I treated the overall journey as the product and the itinerary as a living delivery plan.

### 2. Product vision and value

My product vision was a five-week South American experience anchored in Carnival and designed around contrasts: festival energy, coastal cities, urban culture, Patagonia and waterfalls. The key outcomes were to protect the Carnival experience, minimize avoidable route friction, preserve flexibility where weather and discovery mattered, and return to Berlin through a viable final connection.

Carnival was the non-negotiable anchor; everything else had to work around it. This resembles a fixed-release-date product: the primary value cannot simply be moved to another week if the rest of the plan overruns.

### 3. Discovery: translate ambitions into a candidate route

I began with the main experience and worked outwards. The route shown in my photographic portfolio is Berlin → São Paulo → Rio de Janeiro → Montevideo → Punta del Este → Buenos Aires → Ushuaia → Buenos Aires → Iguazú Falls → Ciudad del Este → São Paulo → Berlin.

I separated **overnight bases** from **day trips**. Punta del Este was a bus excursion from Montevideo, so it provided coastal variety without another hotel change. Ciudad del Este was a day trip during the Iguazú stage, not another overnight base. Buenos Aires appeared twice because returning there after Ushuaia made sense as part of the onward northern route.

This was an early example of a product trade-off: minimize itinerary complexity without removing experiences that meaningfully improved the journey.

### 4. Backlog: make the scope negotiable

I can represent the planning decisions as a product backlog. The highest priorities were the fixed-date Carnival experience, feasible regional sequencing, essential transport, entry/readiness checks and a viable return journey. Rio landmarks, Montevideo and Buenos Aires, Patagonia and Iguazú were high-value features. Individual restaurants, optional side activities and portfolio publication were more flexible.

The backlog is a **retrospective reconstruction** of the decisions visible in the travel story. Priority labels and proposed sprints are illustrative; I do not present them as contemporaneously recorded Scrum metrics.

### 5. Sprint-style delivery model

Scrum normally requires a Scrum Team, timeboxed Sprints and formal events. This was an individual journey, so I use *proposed planning and delivery increments* to explain how Scrum principles apply without inventing a Scrum Master, Developers, ceremony records or velocity.

**Sprint 0 — Discovery and product goal.** I clarified the reason for the trip, the approximate five-week constraint and the desired contrast between Carnival, cities and nature. **Increment:** a product vision and initial ordered backlog.

**Sprint 1 — Route architecture.** I sequenced São Paulo, Rio, Uruguay, Argentina, Patagonia, Iguazú, Paraguay and the Brazilian return. I avoided adding hotel moves when a day trip was enough. **Increment:** a coherent route with key dependencies and travel modes.

**Sprint 2 — Travel readiness.** The planning work would prioritize fixed-date Carnival arrangements, longer-distance legs, accommodation, border requirements and contingency for the homebound flight. Exact booking dates, actual prices and reservation confirmations are not supplied by the portfolio, so I do not invent them. **Increment:** an executable travel baseline.

**Sprint 3 — Deliver the Carnival anchor.** I started in São Paulo for three nights and continued to Rio for twelve days. Rio was deliberately more than a brief Carnival stop. The extended stay also created space for Christ the Redeemer, the Ipanema coastline, its sunset and a memorable buffet discovery. **Increment:** the trip's primary value plus additional city experiences.

**Sprint 4 — Deliver the regional contrast.** I moved to Montevideo for three days. I reached Punta del Este by intercity bus as a day trip, preserving one accommodation base. I then continued via Colonia del Sacramento by bus and ferry to Buenos Aires, where I spent three days. The arrival photograph captures the skyline from the ferry. **Increment:** Uruguay's city-and-coast chapter and the first Argentina stay.

**Sprint 5 — Deliver the southern and northern adventures.** I travelled to Ushuaia for three days. Highlights in the existing portfolio include its waterfront shipwreck, the Laguna Esmeralda hike and a Beagle Channel journey with its lighthouse and penguin colonies. I then returned to Buenos Aires for three more days. The route continued north to Iguazú Falls, with a day across the border to Ciudad del Este, before a final three-night stop in São Paulo and the flight back to Berlin. **Increment:** the remaining high-value experiences and completion of the route.

**Sprint 6 — Review and publish.** I can now inspect the journey through the photos, route and design decisions already recorded in my travel portfolio, and translate them into backlog, sprint, risk and retrospective artifacts. **Increment:** this independent Product Owner case study, linked to the original visual story.

These stages are a teaching model for my portfolio; the travel days should not be misrepresented as historical, formally timeboxed Sprints.

### 6. How I managed dependencies and trade-offs

The central dependency was Carnival. It influenced the order of cities and the amount of time available elsewhere. Longer connections were handled as route-defining legs, while shorter excursions were designed around stable accommodation bases. The Punta del Este day trip reduced packing and hotel changes. The return to Buenos Aires after Ushuaia supported the northbound continuation. Finally, São Paulo was both the opening and the closing Brazilian stage, keeping the international departure at the end of a deliberate route.

I also had to balance a schedule with discovery. Twelve days in Rio allowed both preplanned landmarks and moments I could not specify in advance, such as returning to Ipanema at sunset. In product terms, the plan reserved capacity for emergent value rather than treating every unscheduled hour as waste.

### 7. Practical risk management

| Risk | Impact | Sensible response | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Missing the fixed Carnival window | Critical loss of primary value | Make dates and Rio arrival top priority | Date anchor supported; booking details not documented |
| Delayed cross-border transport | Lost time or onward connection | Allow connection buffers and alternative routes | Route and ferry leg documented; actual buffers not documented |
| Weather affecting Laguna Esmeralda or Beagle Channel | Missed outdoor experiences | Protect flexibility and identify alternative activities | Experiences documented; contingency use unverified |
| Overloaded route | Travel fatigue and less destination time | Combine day trips with overnight bases; prioritize experiences | Punta del Este and Ciudad del Este day trips documented |
| Disruption before international departure | Risk to flight home | Keep a final São Paulo stage | Three final nights documented; precise flight buffer unverified |

I distinguish **design responses** from **events known to have happened**; the portfolio does not establish that a particular disruption occurred.

### 8. Inspecting the result

The trip delivered the breadth visible in the portfolio: the Carnival anchor, Rio city experiences, Montevideo and Punta del Este, two Buenos Aires stays, Ushuaia and Laguna Esmeralda, Iguazú Falls, Ciudad del Este and the return through São Paulo. What is not available from the published evidence is an audited budget, quantitative sprint metrics, booking timestamps or a contemporary change log. I therefore avoid invented claims such as a percentage budget saving, sprint velocity or on-time delivery rate.

The most important result was that the journey retained a clear primary purpose while still making room for different kinds of experiences. Carnival provided the product goal; careful routing protected its delivery; and thoughtful scope choices gave each region a distinct role.

### 9. Retrospective: what I would carry into the next project

**Keep:** start with the single most valuable experience; plan fixed-date dependencies first; group nearby experiences into efficient bases; allow time for discovery instead of optimizing only the number of stops.

**Improve:** maintain a live cost baseline, booking-dependency tracker, explicit contingency allowances and brief decision notes. These would turn a strong travel design into an even more auditable project record.

**Experiment:** for a future journey, use a simple rolling 48-hour plan and quick review at each major destination, then record what changed and why. This borrows Scrum's inspection-and-adaptation mindset without claiming that personal travel is identical to software delivery.

### 10. What this case demonstrates

As Product Owner, I can articulate an outcome-led vision, distinguish non-negotiables from optional scope, prioritize value under constraints, plan around dependencies, make pragmatic trade-offs and communicate decisions. Together with my [public-sector transformation PMO](https://github.com/sumitmilan6/public-sector-digital-transformation-pmo), this case demonstrates both **portfolio-level governance** and **product-level prioritization** through independently developed portfolio work.
